Abstract: This document describes extensions to the Hamiltonian dynamic parser that bridge computational linguistics and hypergraph knowledge representation systems like OpenCog Hyperon and AtomSpace. The core insight: parse trees discovered through energy-minimizing substitution naturally form hypergraph structures where cognitive objects (substitution classes) and cognitive distances (energy landscapes) emerge from the process itself rather than being pre-specified. We present three complementary visualizations—Graph View, Corpus View, and Containment View—that expose different aspects of how hierarchical structure emerges from sequential corpus data. These visualizations inform a path toward dynamic knowledge representation where logical elements are generated at the point of use, grounded in distributional evidence, rather than retrieved from fixed ontologies. This work builds on the core parser documentation and the hypergraph analysis paper.

1. Motivation: From Static Ontologies to Dynamic Structure

1.1 The Knowledge Representation Problem

Traditional hypergraph knowledge representation systems like OpenCog's AtomSpace face a fundamental challenge: structure must be authored. Whether by programmers, by logical inference, or by machine learning, someone or something must decide which nodes exist and which edges connect them. The Hyperon project's Transweave framework identifies the core transfer learning problem as finding representations that capture task-relevant similarities—but this presumes the system knows what constitutes a "relevant similarity" before it encounters the task.

The Hamiltonian dynamic parser offers a fundamentally different approach: structure emerges from energy minimization over distributional evidence. No pre-specified ontology is required. The "objects" (substitution classes) and their relationships (energy differences) are discovered dynamically at the point of use.

Core Thesis: Meaningful cognitive objects are inherently context-dependent and contradictory. The word "animals" in "cats are animals" admits substitutes like mammals, creatures, predators—broad category terms. But "animals" in "animals eat grass" admits a different class: cows, horses, sheep—herbivores. Any system that tries to learn a single, fixed category for "animals" must lose one meaning to preserve the other. Dynamic parsing—discovering substitution classes at the point of use—is not a workaround but the correct response to the irreducible ambiguity of meaning.

1.2 From Parse Trees to Hypergraphs

A parse tree is already a hypergraph. Each non-terminal node (span) is a hyperedge connecting its children. But the parser goes further: each span also connects to its substitution class—the set of alternative phrases that can replace it in context. This creates a rich, multi-layered graph where:

  • Hierarchical edges (parent→children splits) show compositional structure
  • Substitution edges (span→substitutes) show functional equivalence classes
  • Sequential edges (word→word adjacency in corpus) ground everything in observed data

The hypergraph is not imposed on the data—it is extracted from the sequential corpus through energy minimization.

2. The Three Views

To make the emergence of hypergraph structure visible, we implemented three interactive visualization modes. Each exposes a different aspect of how hierarchical relationships, substitution classes, and sequential corpus data interact.

2.1 Graph View: Force-Directed Hypergraph

Purpose: Show parse structure as a network of nodes and edges, styled as an AtomSpace-compatible hypergraph.

Visual Elements:

  • Span nodes (large circles): Each multi-word phrase in the parse tree. Node size encodes the number of substitutes (larger = richer substitution class). Node color encodes energy (green = low/good, yellow = medium, red = high/poor).
  • Leaf nodes (small circles): Single words from the input phrase.
  • Substitute nodes (tiny circles, light blue): Top substitutes for each span, hidden by default—click a span to reveal its substitution class.
  • Split edges (thick solid lines): Binary splits showing the parse tree structure (parent→left child, parent→right child).
  • Substitution edges (thin dashed lines): Connect spans to their top substitutes.

Interaction: Drag nodes to reposition, zoom/pan, click spans to toggle substitute visibility, hover for tooltips showing energy, substitute count, and top contexts.

Implementation: D3.js v7 force-directed layout with custom forces:

forceLink (split edges: strength 1.5, substitute edges: strength 0.3)
forceManyBody (repulsion: -300)
forceY (hierarchy: y position based on tree depth)
forceCollide (no overlap)
Example (parse tree for "we are ok"):
Top-level span node "we are ok" (E=-9.32, 1543 substitutes) splits into:
- Left child: "we are" (E=-11.08, 2156 subs)
- Right child: "ok" (leaf, no energy)

Clicking "we are" reveals substitutes like "you 're", "they 're", "i 'm" (copula + subject variations).
The energy gradient (green→yellow) shows that "we are" is a better-defined unit (lower energy, more substitutes) than the full phrase.

2.2 Corpus View: Sequential Grounding

Purpose: Show how hierarchical parse structure emerges from and is grounded in sequential corpus data.

Visual Elements:

  • Nested arcs (top): One arc per parse span, positioned above the input phrase, colored distinctly, with energy labels. Nesting shows the hierarchy (inner arcs = deeper substructures).
  • Input phrase (middle): Horizontal row of word boxes—the sequential "corpus" representation.
  • Substitute rows (bottom): For each parse span, top substitutes shown as horizontal sequences with corpus context words on either side (grey), connected by dashed curved lines back to their parent span.

Key Insight: The arcs (hierarchical structure) are abstractions discovered from the sequential data below. Each substitute is shown in its actual corpus context (e.g., "... borrow | your towel | and ..."), making visible that substitution is grounded in distributional evidence, not logical rules.

Example (parse for "I found my hat"):
Arc hierarchy (top to bottom):
1. "I found my hat" (E=-12.66) — full phrase
2. "found my hat" (E=-7.21) — nested inside
3. "my hat" (E=-5.84) — deepest nesting

Substitute rows show:
- "found my hat" → "he'd seen the telegram" | corpus: "... said | he'd seen the telegram | in ..."
- "found my hat" → "you've reached your limits" | corpus: "... can, | you've reached your limits | on ..."
- "my hat" → "your towel" | corpus: "... borrow | your towel | and ..."
- "my hat" → "his lessons" | corpus: "... learned | his lessons | quickly ..."

The corpus contexts (grey words) prove these substitutions are empirically grounded, not hallucinated.

2.3 Containment View: Hierarchical Decomposition with Sequential Threads

Purpose: Invert the graph representation—show parent-child relationships as spatial containment rather than external edges. Reveal how substitutes decompose according to parse structure and how sequential links thread through the hierarchy.

Visual Elements:

  • Nested circles (containment regions): Each multi-word span is a circle containing its children. Outer circles = higher-level spans, inner circles = subspans. Circle border color matches energy (color gradient), with energy label on the border.
  • Word nodes (bold blue circles): Input phrase words, positioned inside their deepest containing span.
  • Substitute nodes (small colored circles): Decomposed substitutes positioned near the input word they replace. Critically, multi-word substitutes are split according to the parse structure—e.g., if "found my hat" splits into "found" | "my hat", then substitute "dropped a pencil" splits the same way: "dropped" (substitute for "found") and "a pencil" (substitute for "my hat").
  • Sequential links:
    • Input sequence (bold blue lines with arrows): Threads I→found→my→hat through the nested circles.
    • Substitute sequences (thin dashed lines): Connect decomposed substitute parts in order, e.g., dropped→a→pencil.

Key Algorithmic Contribution: Recursive substitution decomposition. For each span with binary split (left_words | right_words), each substitute is split at the same position. The parts are passed down to child spans, where they are split again if the child also has a split. At leaf level, single-word substitute parts appear as nodes positioned near their corresponding input word. This ensures structural coherence: the substitution class of a composition is built from the substitution classes of its parts.

Example (parse for "(found (my hat))"):

Span "found my hat" (3 words = 1 + 2) has substitute "dropped a pencil":
- Split at same position: "dropped" (1 word) | "a pencil" (2 words)
- Left part "dropped" → assigned as substitute near leaf word "found"
- Right part "a pencil" → inherited into "my hat" circle

Span "my hat" (2 words = 1 + 1) has inherited substitute "a pencil":
- Split: "a" (1 word) | "pencil" (1 word)
- "a" → substitute near leaf word "my"
- "pencil" → substitute near leaf word "hat"

Substitute sequence threading: dropped → a → pencil (dashed line with arrows), visually showing the decomposed substitute parts in order.

If another substitute "found your wallet" appears, it decomposes as:
- "found" = input word (not shown as substitute, redundant)
- "your wallet" → inherited, then split: "your" near "my", "wallet" near "hat"

Result: All substitute parts are positioned according to the input word they replace, creating a left-to-right ordering that respects the sequential structure while showing the hierarchical decomposition.

Implementation: D3.js circle packing (d3.pack()) with custom position-based sorting to respect left-to-right text order. Substitute decomposition implemented as recursive tree traversal with inherited substitutes passed from parent to children.

3. Mapping to Hypergraph Knowledge Representation

3.1 Comparison with OpenCog AtomSpace

Aspect AtomSpace (Traditional) Dynamic Parser (This Work)
Knowledge Nodes Pre-authored atoms (ConceptNode, PredicateNode, etc.) Emergent substitution classes from corpus evidence
Edges/Links Explicitly created (InheritanceLink, SimilarityLink, HebbianLink) Split edges (compositional structure) + substitution edges (functional equivalence) discovered via energy minimization
Attention ECAN: STI/LTI spread along HebbianLinks (symmetric) Energy-based: low-energy spans attract attention (computed from substitutional evidence)
Hierarchical Structure Fixed type hierarchy or logic-based inference Bottom-up CKY: shorter spans first, compositional
Context Dependence Single truth value per link (or context-tagged links) Substitution class varies with context—same word has different classes in different parse positions
Grounding Requires grounded symbol binding or sensor integration Directly grounded in corpus—every substitute verified against observed data

3.2 Asymmetric Containment vs. Symmetric Similarity

A key difference: the parser uses asymmetric containment scoring:

containment(A → B) = |contexts(A) ∩ contexts(B)| / |contexts(A)|

This measures what fraction of A's contexts are contained in B's context set. If A is specific (few contexts) and B is general (many contexts), and all of A's contexts are a subset of B's, then containment(A → B) is high but containment(B → A) is low. This naturally captures taxonomic relationships (dog→animal) without explicit ontological engineering.

In contrast, ECAN's HebbianLinks are symmetric: co-activation strength from A to B equals B to A. The parser demonstrates that substitution itself is asymmetric—"dog" can substitute for "animal" in some contexts (all dog contexts are animal contexts), but not vice versa.

Implication for Hyperon: Replace symmetric HebbianLinks with asymmetric ContainmentLinks. This would allow taxonomic and subsumption relationships to emerge from corpus statistics rather than requiring explicit InheritanceLinks.

3.3 Energy as Attention Allocation

The parser's energy function:

E = -log(num_subs) + log(consensus) - log(num_full)

Three terms:

  • -log(num_subs): More substitutes → lower energy. A span replaceable by many alternatives occupies a clearly defined functional role.
  • +log(consensus): Higher consensus (similar contexts) → higher energy. Penalizes over-constrained classes; rewards substitution classes that appear in diverse contexts.
  • -log(num_full): More full-length substitutes → lower energy. Rewards structural productivity (substitutes that match the span's word count are better compositional units).

This energy function is attention allocation. Low-energy spans are important—they are well-defined, productive units with rich substitution classes. High-energy spans are tentative, poorly supported, or over-specific. Unlike ECAN (which spreads importance along edges), the parser computes importance for structural configurations—proposed ways of decomposing a span into left and right constituents.

Implication for Hyperon: Implement attention allocation as energy minimization over compositional structures. Compute energy for candidate decompositions using substitutional evidence from a corpus or knowledge base. Minimum-energy decomposition defines the preferred interpretation.

4. Continual Learning via Corpus Fallback

4.1 Two-Tier Architecture

The parser maintains two knowledge stores:

  • UnitCatalog (Tier 1): Pre-computed context patterns for high-frequency n-grams, with GPU tensors for fast batch cosine similarity. This is "compiled knowledge."
  • CorpusIndex (Tier 2): Runtime fallback to raw corpus via n-gram index (up to 7-grams, no frequency threshold), with LRU caching for frequently accessed patterns.

The get_unit_with_fallback function bridges these tiers: try catalog first, fall back to corpus if not found.

4.2 Mapping to AtomSpace

  • UnitCatalog ~ Local AtomSpace: Pre-indexed, GPU-accelerated, consolidated patterns for fast cognitive processing.
  • CorpusIndex ~ Distributed AtomSpace / Raw Data Store: Persistent storage queried when local knowledge is insufficient. LRU cache = working memory: frequently needed patterns promoted to fast local access.

The key insight: the parser demonstrates when to fall back to raw data. Whenever a Cartesian product combination (left_substitute × right_substitute) is not in the catalog, the system checks the corpus. Novel combinations can still be evaluated if they appear in raw perceptual data. This is a concrete model for continual learning: handling genuinely novel inputs by accessing perceptual history, not just pre-compiled abstractions.

4.3 Dynamic GPU Index Extension

The add_unit_to_gpu_index method shows how newly discovered patterns are promoted to the fast tier—analogous to adding new Atoms to a live AtomSpace. The knowledge graph grows during processing. New patterns discovered during reasoning are immediately available for subsequent reasoning steps within the same cognitive cycle.

Implication for Hyperon: Implement a two-tier knowledge architecture. Local AtomSpace for fast access, distributed store for comprehensive coverage. When a needed pattern is missing from local space, query the distributed store, extract it, and promote to local space dynamically. This enables true continual learning—the system can encounter genuinely novel structures and incorporate them on the fly.

5. Dynamic Logic Generation

5.1 Compositional Verification

Traditional logic systems (including Hyperon's PLN) compute composite truth values from component truths via logical rules. The parser takes a fundamentally different approach: empirical verification.

When forming Cartesian products of left × right substitutes, each combination is validated against the corpus. Only corpus-attested combinations survive. The truth of "borrow your towel" as a substitute for "found my hat" is not deduced from component truths—it is verified by checking whether "borrow your towel" actually appears in the corpus with compatible contexts.

This is more grounded than deductive composition. It prevents the system from hallucinating structures that are logically consistent but empirically absent.

5.2 Multi-Component Scoring

After verification, the parser applies multi-dimensional scoring:

  • Context path counting: Number of bridging paths between left and right substitutes (corpus co-occurrence)
  • Inner boundary diversity: Variety of words at the split boundary
  • Outer context diversity: Variety of contexts in which the full substitute appears
  • Outer context consensus: Similarity of those contexts (not too diverse—needs coherent functional role)

These go beyond simple truth-value composition. They capture how well a proposed composition is supported by distributional structure in the data.

5.3 Generating Logical Elements Dynamically

The parser does not retrieve pre-compiled logical rules. Instead, it generates "logical elements" (valid compositional structures) dynamically at the point of use, guided by the energy function and grounded in corpus evidence. Each parse is, in effect, a discovered logical structure—a way of decomposing meaning that is supported by substitutional evidence.

In Hyperon terms: rather than authoring MeTTa rules and executing them, the parser discovers valid graph transformations by testing which decompositions minimize energy. The rules are implicit in the energy function and the corpus statistics.

Implication for Hyperon: Implement a mode where MeTTa transformations are not pre-authored but discovered dynamically. Given a complex Atom and a corpus or knowledge base, generate candidate decompositions, score them by energy (substitutional evidence), and select the minimum-energy structure. This enables reasoning without explicit rule engineering—rules emerge from data.

6. Corpus as Hypergraph Substrate

6.1 The Sequential Foundation

A sequential corpus is already a degenerate hypergraph: each word is a node, each bigram adjacency is an edge. It's a path graph—the simplest possible structure.

But the parser's contribution is to discover that certain subsequences form units—they can be grouped and replaced as a whole. This is the transition from a 1D chain to a higher-dimensional structure:

  • Raw corpus: ... I found my hat again ... — a flat chain of adjacency links
  • After parsing: (I ((found (my hat)) again)) — hierarchical groupings imposed on that chain, where each grouping is justified by substitutional evidence drawn from other parts of the same chain

The parse tree is a higher-order structure discovered within the sequential substrate. The groupings are hyperedges—they connect multiple sequential elements into a single unit with properties (substitution class, energy) that individual elements don't have.

6.2 Sequence + Hierarchy Coexist

The Containment View makes this coexistence visible. Sequential links (word→word adjacency) thread through nested containment circles (hierarchical spans). The sequence isn't replaced by the hierarchy—it remains as the foundation. The hierarchy is what the parser extracts from it.

This is the strongest argument for this approach over traditional hypergraph systems: in Hyperon, graph structure is typically authored (by rules, by programmers, by logical inference). In the parser, graph structure emerges from sequential perceptual data via energy minimization. The corpus-as-sequence is the raw material; the hypergraph is what gets extracted.

Implication for Hyperon: Ground all knowledge representation in a sequential substrate—whether text corpora, sensory streams, or action sequences. Use energy minimization over distributional statistics to discover which subsequences form meaningful units. This grounds the hypergraph in perceptual data rather than requiring explicit structure authoring.

9. Future Directions

9.1 Incremental Parsing with Hypergraph Updates

Current implementation: batch parsing (full phrase at once). Future: incremental parsing where each new word triggers local hypergraph updates. This would model human sentence processing more accurately and enable real-time reasoning.

9.2 Multi-Corpus Federated Learning

Current: single corpus. Future: multiple corpora (different domains, languages, modalities) with distributed parsing. Substitution classes discovered in one corpus can inform parsing in another, enabling transfer learning grounded in distributional evidence.

9.3 Integration with Hyperon MeTTa

Concrete implementation path:

  1. Export parse structures as MeTTa atoms: (ParseSpan "found my hat" (Split "found" "my hat") (Energy -7.21) (Substitutes ...))
  2. Implement energy function as MeTTa grounded operation: (energy-of $span) queries corpus, computes score
  3. CKY bottom-up process as MeTTa rewrite rules: shorter spans → longer spans, minimum energy wins
  4. Substitution verification as MeTTa query: (corpus-attested? $phrase $left-ctx $right-ctx)

Result: hypergraph knowledge representation where structure is discovered dynamically, grounded in corpus evidence, with energy-driven attention allocation.

9.4 Visual Query Interface

Extend the visualizations to support interactive queries: click a word or span, see all corpus contexts where it appears; click two spans, see bridging paths (shared contexts); drag a new substitute into a span, compute energy in real-time to evaluate fit.

10. Conclusion

The hypergraph extensions demonstrate that the Hamiltonian dynamic parser's energy-minimizing substitution mechanism naturally generates hypergraph structures where:

  • Nodes (cognitive objects) are substitution classes that vary with context
  • Edges (relationships) are compositional splits (parent→children) and functional equivalences (span→substitutes)
  • Attention (importance) is energy-based: low-energy structures attract attention because they are well-supported by distributional evidence
  • Grounding is direct: every structure is validated against observed corpus data
  • Learning is continual: novel structures can be evaluated on the fly by falling back to raw data

The three visualization modes—Graph View, Corpus View, Containment View—expose complementary aspects of this emergent structure. They show how hierarchical relationships, sequential data, and substitution classes interact to form a rich, multi-layered hypergraph that is extracted from data rather than imposed on it.

This approach offers a path toward knowledge representation systems that do not require explicit ontological engineering, that generate logical elements dynamically at the point of use, and that remain grounded in distributional evidence throughout. The parser demonstrates that structure, meaning, and reasoning can emerge from a single principle: energy minimization over substitutional evidence.
